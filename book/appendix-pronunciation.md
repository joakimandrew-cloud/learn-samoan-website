# Pronunciation Guide {.unnumbered}

This guide gathers the sound system of Samoan in one place, for looking things up once the Introduction is behind you. Nothing here changes what the Introduction taught; where this guide goes further, it adds the detail that makes sense only after you have been saying Samoan aloud for a while. Every word in it is one this book teaches, and every sound it describes is in the careful style the book teaches, unless a section says otherwise.

---

## The Vowels

Samoan has five vowels, a, e, i, o and u, and each of them can be short or long. No difference between the two speech styles has been found in their vowels: whatever you learn here about vowels holds for the everyday style as well. A change of vowel alone is enough to make a different word, as in *tā* (strike) and *tū* (stand).

The five are simple, steady sounds. The i is said with the tongue high and at the front of the mouth and the lips spread; the u with the tongue high at the back and the lips rounded; the a with the mouth open and the tongue low and central. The e and the o sit between those, e toward i and o toward u.

At the end of a word, e and o close up a little, so that a final e drifts toward i and a final o toward u. You can hear it in *tele* (big, many), whose last vowel is a closed e. In Samoan a word ending in e rhymes with one ending in i, and the same holds for o and u, which is why the Introduction warned that a final e and a final i are hard to tell apart by ear.

Two more habits belong to vowels that carry no stress. An unstressed a often loosens, with the mouth a little less open. An unstressed i at the start of a word, with another vowel after it, tends to slide into a y sound, and an unstressed u in the same place into a w sound.

## Long Vowels

A long vowel is, usually, the same sound as its short partner held for longer. Long e and long o at the end of a word sometimes glide toward a closer vowel as they finish, but the core of the sound does not change.

Length is what changes a word, as the Introduction showed. Measured as sound, there seem to be more than two lengths: a vowel may be extra short, short, of normal length, long, or extra long, depending on where it stands and whether it carries the stress. Those five sort into the two that change meaning, short and long, and those two are what the long-vowel mark writes. The first vowel of *nonofo* (stay, sit, said of more than one) is one of the extra short kind, so short that in fast speech it can disappear altogether.

Long vowels normally carry stress. It fits that a word with a long vowel at its end, like *tamā* (father), is stressed on that last vowel, as the next section explains.

Two things happen to vowels across the gap between words. A short vowel at the end of a word is usually dropped when the next word begins with a vowel. And when a word ends in the same vowel the next word begins with, the two often run together into a single long vowel. Neither is a spelling rule: the words are written in full, and this book prints them that way. They are what you will often hear in running speech.

## Where the Stress Falls

Every Samoan word stem has one syllable that carries the main stress. In most Samoan words it is the second-to-last syllable. Most of the time a syllable is one vowel, so counting back two vowels finds the stress. The table shows the habit at work, and the words where it needs a second look.

| Samoan | English | The stress falls on |
|---|---|---|
| *tama* | boy | the first a |
| *fale* | house | the a |
| *nonofo* | stay, sit (more than one) | the second o |
| *taeao* | morning | the second a: each of the four vowels is a syllable |
| *tamā* | father | the final ā, because it is long |
| *maile* | dog | the ai, said together as one syllable |
| *teine* | girl | the ei, said together as one syllable |
| *falaoa* | bread | the la, one syllable further back than the habit predicts |

Two vowels side by side are usually two syllables, and each counts on its own, as in *taeao*. A few pairs behave differently when they stand in that second-to-last place: ei, eu, ai, au, ou, oi, and sometimes ui are said as one syllable there, and the pair carries the stress together, as in *maile* and *teine*. At the very end of a word the same pairs go back to counting as two.

When the last vowel of a word is long, it draws the stress to itself, as in *tamā*. Words like this are common.

Read those as habits rather than as laws. Only two stems seem to be stressed a syllable further back than the habit predicts: *falaoa*, and the name of a village on Tutuila. And some word endings pull the stress to a place of their own. The clearest is the ending *=ina*: a word that carries it is always stressed on its i. So take each new word slowly, find its stress by ear, and say it aloud rather than working it out from the spelling.

## The ʻ Mark

The ʻ mark stands for a consonant, a catch in the voice, and it counts as fully as an f or a t. When it is written at all, it is written as ʻ; this book writes it every time.

At the start of a word the ʻ is a clean catch, the voice stopped and let go. Between two vowels it can be the same catch, or it can be heard as a short stretch of creaky voice, the vowels carrying on through a string of tiny catches.

The ʻ is unsteady in running speech, in three ways:

- At the start of a word it is often lost, except in very careful speech. Many speakers now treat some short words without it as the normal form: *ʻe* (you, one person) is one of them. This book still prints the mark, and you should still say it.
- The opposite happens too. Any vowel at the start of a word can pick up a catch of its own when it is said with emphasis, or at the start of a phrase, so you will sometimes hear a catch where none is written.
- Between two identical vowels the catch is often dropped in fast speech, especially when neither vowel carries the main stress, and the two vowels can then run together into one long vowel.

You can hear the first of these in the change particle *ʻua*. Outside careful speech it often loses its catch, and its u then slides into a w sound. If the catch had stayed, the u could not have done that.

None of this changes what you say. Learn every word with its ʻ, say the catch every time, and let your ear get used to hearing it come and go in other people's speech.

## The Long-Vowel Mark, and the Marks in Print

The line over a long vowel is the long-vowel mark.

The first missionary spelling of Samoan wrote neither the length of a vowel nor the catch. From around 1850, printed Samoan began to mark the catch with a raised comma turned the other way and a long vowel with a line over it, but only here and there, mostly where a word could otherwise be read two ways. In that older printing the long-vowel mark was never put over a capital letter, and the ʻ mark was usually left off before one. No fixed rule for either mark ever settled.

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, school books and translated novels began to use both marks much more, and school books and children's books from Pagopago and Honolulu, with some children's books from New Zealand, still do. Even there, each author decides where to put them, and a sentence can mark a word that the next sentence leaves bare. In Western Samoa, the Education Department dropped both marks from its school books in the late 1960s. That unmarked spelling is now the generally accepted one there, in the schools, in the newspapers, and in most books by Samoan authors. The marks come back only now and then, to settle a word that would otherwise be unclear; some modern writers print such a word twice, first bare and then again, marked, in brackets.

The Introduction showed what the bare spelling costs. Here is one more set, to show how far it can go: the three bare letters p, a, u can stand for four different words, meaning "end", "stern, fierce", "skin", and "fall", told apart in speech by length, by the catch, and by stress. Written bare, only the sentence around them decides.

## The Consonants in More Detail

The Introduction gave you the letters. A few of them carry more than their letter suggests.

- The t is usually said with the flat of the tongue against the ridge behind the upper teeth. Before an i, many speakers today say it close to ts; people of every age do this, and it seems most common among young women and children.
- The g is the ng sound, as the Introduction said. In the careful style it seems most often to be made a little further forward in the mouth than the English ng of "song".
- The h comes only at the start of a word, in borrowed words and a handful of exclamations.
- The k, in the careful style, belongs mainly to borrowed words, most of them from English.
- The r belongs only to borrowed words, and even in careful speech it is often said as l. An English r usually becomes l in a borrowed word, as in *leitio* (radio). The one old Samoan word regularly heard with an r is *talo* (taro), and both an l and an r are acceptable there.

## The Everyday Style: t Becomes k

This book teaches the careful style and asks you to produce only that. The everyday style is for recognizing, and this section gathers what you need to recognize it.

The careful style has thirteen consonants and the everyday style has ten. The difference is three mergers, each folding two sounds into one:

- t and k become k,
- n and g (the ng sound) become g,
- r and l become l.

So in everyday speech, words that the careful style keeps apart come out sounding the same. The word for tea and the word for key sound alike; so do the word for cave and the word for conduct, and the word for cocoa and the word for blood. That is the one-way folding the Introduction described: hearing everyday speech, you work back from the merged sound to the careful word you learned.

Two smaller habits go with the mergers. The catch at the start of a word seems to be dropped even more often in the everyday style than in careful speech. Long vowels before the stressed syllable are often cut short. And both can meet the mergers in a single word: *tōfā* (goodbye) can reach you with a k in place of its t and its first vowel cut short, so that only the final ā keeps its length. When you hear a word you almost know, try putting back a t for its k, an n for its g, and a long vowel where a short one went by.
