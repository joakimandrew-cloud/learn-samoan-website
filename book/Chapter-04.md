# Chapter 4: The Tense Particles

Building on the past particle *sā*, this chapter completes the tense line by
introducing the three remaining tense particles: the second past *na*, for a
single finished event; the change particle *ʻua*, for a change that usually
leaves a new state in place; and the future particle *ʻoleʻā*.

---

## The Four Tense Particles

::: {.examples}
*Sā ʻou alu.* I went.

*ʻOleʻā ʻou alu.* I will go.
:::

The frame is the one taught already: a first word that fixes the time, then
the pronoun, then the verb. In the past sentence that first word is *sā*. Swap
it for *ʻoleʻā* and the same going moves into the future. The verb *alu* keeps
its shape, and the pronoun *ʻou* keeps its place. Only the first word changes,
and with it the time of the whole sentence.

That first slot holds a tense particle, and this chapter fills it four ways: the
two pasts *sā* and *na*, the change particle *ʻua*, and the future *ʻoleʻā*. Here
is one sentence run across all four:

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *Sā ʻou alu.* | I went. |
| *Na ʻou alu.* | I went. |
| *ʻUa ʻou alu.* | I have gone. |
| *ʻOleʻā ʻou alu.* | I will go. |

Two of these are pasts, and the plain English "I went" fits both; the
difference between them is the first thing to learn.

---

## The Two Pasts

Samoan has two past particles, and every past sentence chooses between them.
Here is the same verb with each particle:

::: {.examples}
*na alofa*. fell in love (Lit. "past love")

*sā alofa*. loved (Lit. "past love")
:::

Compare them. Which word changed, and does the English change with it?

Only the first word changed, from *na* to *sā*, and the English changed with
it. One is "fell in love", a single bounded event. The other is "loved", a
state that held over time.

The choice is not about how long ago the event was. It is about the shape of the
event. Ask one question of it. Was it a single, bounded event, a thing that
happened once, or a sudden turn you did not see coming? Then use *na*. Was it how
things were, something ongoing, habitual, or the background state of a stretch of
time? Then use *sā*.

*Na* is the past of the single event, and it is typically, though not
exclusively, met with verbs that name a beginning or an end, like *ʻāmata*
(begin):

::: {.examples}
*Na ʻāmata.* It began. (Lit. "past begin")
:::

> *Note:* Nothing in the Samoan names what began; the "it" belongs to the
> English. A doer can be left unsaid like this when what has already been said
> lets your listener work out who or what is meant. That is not the same as the
> weather sentences met earlier, where there is no doer to name at all.

*Sā* is the past of how things were, the states and habits that held over a
stretch of time:

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *Sā ʻou maʻi.* | I was sick. |
| *Sā mātou fiafia.* | We were happy. |

> *Note:* The two pasts overlap at the edges, and you will sometimes hear either
> one where the other would also serve. The question above about the shape of the
> event will carry you through nearly every sentence. Where it does not settle
> the choice, one more difference often does: the second past can report an event
> as unexpected, while the past of how things were reports one that is common and
> nothing out of the ordinary.

---

## The Change Particle *ʻUa*

::: {.examples}
*ʻUa vevela.* It has become hot.
:::

*ʻUa* is the third word for the first slot, and it does a different job from
the two pasts. It does not send an event back in time. It marks a change, and
reports that a new state is usually in place now. *ʻUa vevela* does not
say it was hot once; it says the weather has turned, and hot is how it is
now. Use *ʻua* whenever something has changed and the new state still stands:

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *ʻUa vevela.* | It has become hot. |
| *ʻUa maʻalili.* | It has become cold. |
| *ʻUa ʻou maʻi.* | I have become sick. |
| *ʻUa ʻou pisi.* | I have become busy. |

In each of these the new state is still in place as you speak. That is the usual
case rather than a rule: the particle reports the change, and the state it
leaves need not still hold at the moment you say it.

Compare *ʻua* with *sā*. *Sā* closes a state off in the past; *ʻua* opens a new
state that holds now:

::: {.examples}
*Sā ia tāuaso.* She was blind.

*ʻUa ia vaʻai.* Now she sees.
:::

The first sentence, with *sā*, reports a past state that is over. The second,
with *ʻua*, reports the change and the new state that holds now. Samoan keeps
the two moments apart where English can let them blur.

> **Preview:** These four are the everyday tense particles, the ones that fix an
> event in time. Other words share that same first slot, and one small set of
> them colours a sentence with things like a wish or a gentle command. Those are
> the mood particles, taught in Chapter 48.

---

## The Future Particle *ʻOleʻā*

::: {.examples}
*ʻOleʻā ia sau.* She will come.
:::

The fourth word for the first slot, *ʻoleʻā*, places the event ahead of now. It
marks something expected to happen, usually quite soon. It fills the slot exactly
as the others do:

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *ʻOleʻā ʻou alu.* | I will go. |
| *ʻOleʻā ia sau.* | She will come. |
| *ʻOleʻā mātou siva.* | We will dance. |

*ʻOleʻā* has a shorter form, *ʻā*, that carries the same meaning. You will hear
the short form especially in questions, as in *ʻĀ sau?* for "Is she coming?"
(Lit. "will come?")

---

## Words to Learn

**New grammar words** (memorize how these work):

| Word | What it does |
|---|---|
| *na* | the second past: marks a single, finished, or unexpected event |
| *ʻua* | the change particle: says something has changed and a new state is usually in place now |
| *ʻoleʻā* | the future particle: places the event ahead of now (short form *ʻā*) |

**New vocabulary** (memorize these meanings). Most of these verbs name a
state, and they sit naturally with *ʻua* to report that the state has just set
in:

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *ʻāmata* | begin, start |
| *maʻi* | sick |
| *tāuaso* | blind |
| *vaʻai* | see |
| *vevela* | hot |
| *maʻalili* | cold |
| *pisi* | busy |

**Words to carry** (learned now, used fully later in this book): *fai* is one
of the most common verbs in Samoan, covering "do", "make", "say", and
"tell", but it leans on words for the thing done or said, which come later
in this book.

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *fai* | do, make, say, tell |
