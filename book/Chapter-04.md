# Chapter 4: The Two Pasts

This chapter introduces the second past particle *na* and teaches one rule for
choosing between the two pasts, *na* for something that happened and *sā* for
how things were, and shows you how to test any past sentence against it.

---

## The Two Pasts

::: {.examples}
*na nofo*. sat down (Lit. "past sit")

*sā nofo*. was sitting, stayed (Lit. "past sit")
:::

Compare them. Which word changed, and what happened to the English?

Only the first word changed, from *na* to *sā*, and the English changed with
it. "Sat down" is one event, over in a moment, and "was sitting" is a stretch
of time. *Na* is the **second past particle**. It goes in the same first slot as
*sā*, in front of the pronoun and the verb, and a past sentence takes one of
the two. One rule decides which:

| Particle | The rule |
|---|---|
| *na* | It happened. One event, and you could point to the moment. |
| *sā* | That's how it was. A stretch of time back then: a state, something going on, or a regular pattern. No single moment is in view. |

To choose, put the English in a sentence. If "at that moment", "suddenly" or
"that one time" fits, the past is *na*. If "back then", "all that time" or "in
those days" fits, the past is *sā*. How long ago it happened does not decide
the choice; the test does.

| *Na*: it happened | *Sā*: that's how it was |
|---|---|
| *na nofo*: sat down (at that moment) | *sā nofo*: was sitting, stayed (all that time) |
| *na alofa*: fell in love (it began) | *sā alofa*: loved (that's how it was) |
| *na iloa*: noticed (the moment it clicked) | *sā iloa*: knew (the knowing went on) |
| *Na ʻou maʻi.* I fell sick. (at that moment) | *Sā ʻou maʻi.* I was sick. (all that time) |
| *Na ʻou fāgota.* I went fishing. (that one trip) | *Sā ʻou fāgota.* I used to go fishing, I was fishing. (in those days) |

In every row the verb keeps its shape and only the particle moves. The English
in this book is chosen to show the difference. A *na* line gets English that
names the event: "sat down", "fell sick", "went fishing". A *sā* line gets
English that shows a stretch of time: "was sitting", "was sick", "used to go
fishing". Plain English could say "I went fishing" for both, and then the
difference would be lost.

### *Sā* Is About Back Then

*Sā* is a past. A *sā* sentence describes back then, and it makes no statement
about today. English works the same way. "In those days I went fishing every
Saturday" tells you about those days. It doesn't tell you whether you still
go, because the sentence isn't about now. So *Sā ʻou fāgota*, "I used to go
fishing", never means "I used to go fishing, and I still go". It is a sentence
about back then, and nothing more.

To talk about how things are now, Sāmoan uses a different particle, the change
particle, taught in a later chapter. A story about a woman who was blind and
can now see says it in two parts: "She was blind", with *sā*, about back then,
and "but now she sees", with the change particle, about now.

## States and How They Start

::: {.examples}
*Na ʻou maʻi.* I fell sick.

*Sā ʻou maʻi.* I was sick.
:::

These sentences show the rule on a word for a state. *Maʻi* (sick) can name
the moment a sickness began or the sickness itself, and the particle tells the
listener which. With *na* it can name the start: at that moment, I fell sick.
With *sā* it names the state: all that time, I was sick. *Ala* (wake up, be
awake) and *iloa* (know, notice) work the same way:

| *Na*: the moment it began | *Sā*: how it was |
|---|---|
| *Na ʻou ala.* I woke up. (at that moment) | *Sā ʻou ala.* I was awake. (all that time) |
| *Na ʻou iloa.* I noticed. (the moment it clicked) | *Sā ʻou iloa.* I knew. (the knowing went on) |
| *Na lātou iloa.* They noticed. (at that moment) | *Sā lātou iloa.* They knew. (back then) |

> *Note:* With *na*, a word for a state can name the start, as in every row
> above. What is said around the sentence confirms which reading is meant.

A state told as how things were takes *sā*:

::: {.examples}
*Sā ia tāuaso.* She was blind.

*Sā mātou fiafia.* We were happy.
:::

*Tāuaso* means "blind". Said of the woman who was blind for a long time and
can now see, *Sā ia tāuaso* describes how things were back then. Being happy
works the same way: back then, that's how it was.

## Things Going On

::: {.examples}
*Na ʻou nofo.* I sat down.

*Sā ʻou nofo.* I stayed.
:::

These sentences show the rule on something that goes on for a while. *Nofo*
means "sit" or "stay". With *na* it is the moment of sitting down: at that
moment, I sat down. With *sā* it is the sitting or staying that went on: all
that time, I stayed.

| Sāmoan | English | The test |
|---|---|---|
| *Na mātou nofo.* | We sat down. | at that moment |
| *Sā mātou nofo.* | We stayed. | all that time |
| *Na mātou siva.* | We danced. | that one time |
| *Sā mātou siva.* | We were dancing. | all that time |

English "was ...ing" and "stayed" show a *sā* line of this kind. A simple past
that names the event, "sat down" or "danced", shows a *na* line.

## Habits

::: {.examples}
*Na ʻou fāgota.* I went fishing.

*Sā ʻou fāgota.* I used to go fishing.
:::

These sentences show the rule on a habit. One fishing trip is one event: that
one time, I went fishing. A man in a Sāmoan story, asked where he has been,
answers in this way, with *na*, because his trip is one event, begun and
finished. Fishing as a regular pattern is how things were: in those days, I
used to go fishing. A habit has no single moment to point to, so it takes
*sā*.

| Sāmoan | English | The test |
|---|---|---|
| *Na lātou alu.* | They went. | that one time |
| *Sā lātou alu.* | They used to go. | in those days |
| *Na mātou fāgota.* | We went fishing. | that one time |
| *Sā mātou fāgota.* | We used to go fishing. | in those days |
| *Sā ʻou tāʻele.* | I used to bathe. | in those days |
| *Sā ia tagi.* | She used to cry. | in those days |

Nothing in these sentences says "every day". The habit comes from what is said
around them, and *sā* is the past that fits it. A sentence saying where
someone's parents used to work takes *sā* in the same way.

## Beginnings and Ends

::: {.examples}
*Na ʻāmata.* It began. (Lit. "past begin")

*Na ʻuma.* It came to an end. (Lit. "past be finished")
:::

> *Note:* Nothing in the Sāmoan names what began; the "it" belongs to the
> English. A doer can be left unsaid like this when what has already been said
> lets your listener work out who or what is meant. That is not the same as the
> weather sentences met earlier, where there is no doer to name at all.

A beginning and an end are moments you can point to, so *na* fits them: at
that moment, it began; at that moment, it came to an end. The verb *ʻāmata*
(begin) names a beginning, and *ʻuma* (be finished) names the end of
something. *Na* is typically, though not exclusively, met with verbs that name
a beginning or an end, like *ʻāmata*. Said of the year the broadcasts first
started, *Na ʻāmata* points at that one beginning.

A beginning that came round again and again is a regular pattern, and a
regular pattern takes *sā*:

::: {.examples}
*Sā ʻāmata.* It used to begin. (Lit. "past begin")
:::

Said of a broadcast that began at the same hour every Saturday night,
*Sā ʻāmata* fits "in those days": back then, that is when it began, week after
week.

## The Moment a Story Turns

A storyteller uses the same rule across a whole story. In one story an old man
and his son get ready one morning to go hunting in the bush, and the
storyteller tells the run of events with *sā*, as the way things went. Then the
old man's mind goes into confusion, and the storyteller switches to *na*: this
is the moment it happened, the point the story turns on. A line soon after, in
which the pig notices the old man's confusion and attacks him, opens with
*na iloa* (noticed).

*Na* sets that moment apart from everything else that happened. When you read
a story, the *sā* lines tell how things went, and the first *na* line is often
the moment to watch.

## Which Past?

Every sentence below goes through the same test. Find the phrase that fits,
and the phrase gives the past.

1. "I sat down." At that moment: *na*. *Na ʻou nofo.*
2. "I stayed." All that time: *sā*. *Sā ʻou nofo.*
3. "I fell sick." At that moment: *na*. *Na ʻou maʻi.*
4. "I was sick." All that time: *sā*. *Sā ʻou maʻi.*
5. "I noticed." At that moment, it clicked: *na*. *Na ʻou iloa.*
6. "I knew." All that time, the knowing went on: *sā*. *Sā ʻou iloa.*
7. "I woke up." Suddenly, at that moment: *na*. *Na ʻou ala.*
8. "I went fishing", one trip. That one time: *na*. *Na ʻou fāgota.*
9. "I used to go fishing." In those days: *sā*. *Sā ʻou fāgota.*
10. "It began", the year the broadcasts started. That one time: *na*. *Na ʻāmata.*
11. "It used to begin", every Saturday night. In those days: *sā*. *Sā ʻāmata.*
12. "She was blind", before she could see. Back then: *sā*. *Sā ia tāuaso.*

---

## Three Uses You Will Meet in Stories

The rule above is how to choose a past. Stories also show three uses that stretch
it, and a reader should recognise them.

### *Na* for a Habit That Ended

Plain "used to" is *sā*. A storyteller can use *na* for a habit when the point
is that the habit is over, and then the English is "used to ..., but that's
over". One story about an ant and a spider, who gathered their food together
every day, opens with *na* for that habit, so the reader knows from the first
line that the habit ended. In the same way, in a story that has already said
the fishing happened every day, *Na mātou fāgota* tells the reader "We used to
go fishing, but that's over". This is the exception: it adds "and that ended"
to a habit, which *sā* does not do.

### *Sā* for One Ordinary Event

::: {.examples}
*Sā paʻū.* She fell down. (Lit. "past fall down")
:::

*Paʻū* means "fall". A girl fell and was soon back on her feet. Her mother,
who had not seen it, asked why she was crying, and someone who had watched
answered *Sā paʻū*. The fall was one quick event, yet the speaker used *sā*,
telling it as nothing unusual. English has only the plain past here, "She fell
down".

### *Na* for a Long Stretch That Had Its Limits

The blind woman's sentence can also be told with *na*: "the girl who was
blind, but can see now". The blindness lasted a long time, and *na* marks it
as limited to a certain time, for all its length. When a speaker wants a long
state marked off as over and done, *na* can do that too.

### Variation

The two pasts show much variation, and many sentences allow either one, so
stories will not always follow the rule. In the hunting story, even the old
man's sudden fright is told with *sā*, and only the turn gets *na*. When you
choose a past yourself, the rule is how to choose.
