# Chapter 1: The Basic Sentence

This chapter introduces a three-part sentence pattern (the past particle, a
preverbal pronoun, the verb) and shows you how to say what someone did and ask
whether something happened. The sentence *Sā timu?* ("Did it rain?") is your
first example of asking whether something happened.

---

## The Pattern

::: {.examples}
*Sā ʻou alu.* I went. (Lit. "Past I go.")

*Sā ia alu.* She went.
:::

These two sentences show the whole pattern of this chapter. Each one has three
parts in a fixed order: first *sā*, then *ʻou* or *ia*, then *alu*. Change the
middle word and you change who did it. Everything else stays where it is.

### The Verb

*Alu* means "go". A **verb** is a word that names an action or a happening, and
in a Samoan sentence it carries the meaning of what was done. *Alu* does not
change its shape to show that the going already happened. English reaches
inside the verb and bends it, so that "go" becomes "went". Samoan leaves the
verb alone: *alu* stays *alu* whether the going is past, present, or still to
come. A separate word carries the time.

### The Past Particle *Sā*

That separate word is *sā*, the **past particle**: the word that goes in front
and stamps the whole sentence as already done. It is the first of the three
parts, and it never changes. Where English bends the verb, Samoan sets *sā*
in front of it and leaves the rest untouched.

> **Preview:** Samoan has a second past particle with a shade of meaning of its
> own, for events seen as single and finished. You meet it in Chapter 4. Every
> past sentence you build in this chapter uses *sā*.

### The Preverbal Pronoun

Between the particle and the verb sits the word for who did it. *ʻOu* means "I".
*Ia* means "he" or "she". A word that stands in for a person in this slot is
called a **preverbal pronoun**, because it stands before the verb. The rest of
the set is taught later in this book; these two are enough to talk about
yourself and one other person.

This chapter has four action words for this frame: *alu* (go), *siva* (dance),
*tāʻele* (bathe), and *fāgota* (go fishing). The three parts combine like this:

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *Sā ʻou alu.* | I went. |
| *Sā ʻou siva.* | I danced. |
| *Sā ʻou tāʻele.* | I bathed. |
| *Sā ʻou fāgota.* | I went fishing. |

Swap the middle word for *ia* and the same four things are said about someone
else:

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *Sā ia alu.* | She went. |
| *Sā ia siva.* | She danced. |
| *Sā ia tāʻele.* | She bathed. |
| *Sā ia fāgota.* | She went fishing. |

The verb sits last in every row, the particle first in every row, and only the
middle word moves. That fixed order is the pattern of this chapter.

---

## Weather Sentences

Sometimes there is no one to name. When rain falls, no person does the falling,
and Samoan says nothing at all in the middle slot:

::: {.examples}
*Sā timu.* It rained.
:::

*Timu* means "to rain". English needs the empty word "it" in "it rained"; Samoan
does not. The particle and the verb alone make a complete, correct sentence,
one of the shortest sentences in this book.

---

## Asking Questions

To turn a statement into a yes or no question, Samoan changes no words at all.
Read these two aloud:

::: {.examples}
*Sā ia alu.* She went.

*Sā ia alu?* ↘ Did she go?
:::

Compare them. Which words changed?

None of them did. The words are identical, and so is their order. What changes
is the tune of the voice, and here Samoan runs the opposite way to English. An
English yes or no question lifts the voice at the end. A Samoan yes or no
question does not lift it.
Instead the voice usually falls, staying low over the last two or three
syllables. The arrow ↘ in this book marks that falling question tune. Say the
statement with the ending kept low, and you have asked a question.

::: {.examples}
*Sā timu?* ↘ Did it rain?

*Sā ia siva?* ↘ Did she dance?
:::

To answer no, you already have the word. *Leai* means "no", and you can use it
on its own from your very first day.

> **Preview:** *Leai* has a grammar of its own, and a story that is told later,
> in Chapter 14, and completed in Chapter 15. Until then, use it exactly as
> English uses "no".

> *Note:* Only questions carry the falling arrow in this book, and only in the
> chapters that teach the question tune. A statement is never marked. When you
> see *Sā ia alu?* ↘ you are being shown how to say it, not how to write it:
> ordinary Samoan writes the question mark and leaves the tune to the voice.

## Words to Learn

**New grammar words** (memorize how these work):

| Word | What it does |
|---|---|
| *sā* | the past particle: puts the whole sentence in the past |
| *ʻou* | I |
| *ia* | he, she |

**New vocabulary** (memorize these meanings):

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *alu* | go |
| *siva* | dance |
| *tāʻele* | bathe, have a shower |
| *fāgota* | go fishing |
| *timu* | to rain (the weather word) |

**Words to carry** (learned now, used fully later in this book): *alofa*
(love) and *lelei* (good, well) describe a feeling and a quality rather than
an action. The word for good does its describing beside another verb, which
is taught in a later chapter; the word for love takes a marker naming who is
loved, and that marker comes later in this book.

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *alofa* | love |
| *lelei* | good, well |
| *leai* | no |
