# Chapter 25: The Possessive System in Full

This chapter completes the two belonging words *a* and *o*, adding the rule of
control that decides between them, the four sets of possessive forms that track
the articles across one thing and many, and the owners that come in pairs and
groups, and the for-word *mo*, so you can speak of anyone's things in any number
and say who a thing is for. Where the control rule runs out, a word's belonging
type is a fact of that word, and this chapter gives the lists.

---

## The Meaning Behind *A* and *O*

::: {.examples}
*ʻO le leitio a le aliʻi.* The radio of the chief.

*ʻO le fale o le aliʻi.* The house of the chief.
:::

One owner, two belonging words. Earlier in this book the sort was a fact to
learn word by word: a knife takes *a*, a head takes *o*. A rule runs underneath
it, and it is a rule about control. Ask one question of the relationship: did
the owner set it going? A chief buys a radio and puts it to use, so the radio
joins to him with *a*. His house is his in another way, the place he belongs to
and lives in rather than a thing he went out and got, so it joins to him with
*o*.

The belonging word *a* marks a relationship the owner started and controls. The
belonging word *o* marks a relationship that is given, one the owner did not set
up. That is the whole rule, and it sorts most of the language:

| The thing | Belonging word | The relationship |
|---|---|---|
| *leitio* (radio) | *a* | got and put to use |
| *naifi* (knife) | *a* | got and put to use |
| *fale* (house lived in) | *o* | given, not set up |
| *ulu* (head) | *o* | part of the person |
| *lanu* (colour) | *o* | a quality of the thing itself |
| *pito* (end) | *o* | part of a whole |

> *Note:* The owner in an *a* phrase is usually a person, because getting a
> thing and putting it to use is something people do. It does not have to be
> one.

---

## What Takes *A* and What Takes *O*

The two words sort into groups, and the groups are worth knowing as groups.
Here are the main ones, with the words of this book that fall under them.

The *o*-words, mostly given rather than set up:

| Group | Words |
|---|---|
| family by birth | *tamā* (father) |
| parts of the body | *tino* (body), *ulu* (head), *lima* (hand), *vae* (leg) |
| a part of a whole | *pito* (end) |
| a thing's own quality | *lanu* (colour) |
| clothes worn | *ʻie* (kilt), *ʻofu* (dress) |
| old tools and weapons | *tao* (spear) |
| boats | *vaʻa* (boat), *paopao* (canoe) |
| where a person lives or comes from | *nuʻu* (village), *atunuʻu* (country), *fale* (house) |
| a name | *igoa* (name) |

The *a*-words, mostly got, made, or taken on:

| Group | Words |
|---|---|
| one's children | *fānau* (children) |
| a mother's son | *tama* (son) |
| a spouse | *āvā* (wife) |
| people taken into a life | *uō* (friend), *pele* (darling), *maʻamusa* (lover) |
| the beard | *ʻava* (beard) |
| things got and used | *naifi* (knife), *ipu* (cup), *sipuni* (spoon), *fana* (gun), *taʻavale* (car), *meaʻai* (food) |

A kilt is worn, and clothes worn are *o*-words. A spoon is got and used, and
things got and used are *a*-words. The possessive forms follow the same sort
they always have:

::: {.examples}
*ʻO loʻu ʻie.* My kilt.

*ʻO laʻu sipuni.* My spoon.
:::

## Where the Rule Runs Out

Read this pair. One relationship, seen from either parent:

::: {.examples}
*ʻO le tama a le fafine.* The son of the woman.

*ʻO le tama o le tamāloa.* The son of the man.
:::

Compare them. Which word changed?

The belonging word, and the owner with it. It is the same person. A mother's son
is joined to her with *a*, and a father's son is joined to him with *o*. Control
does not explain the pair, and no reason for it is known.

That is the honest state of the sort. The control question sorts most words, and
then a set of words sit where it does not put them. Clothes are the
clearest case: you choose a kilt and put it on, which sounds like control, and
the language uses *o*. The beard runs the other way, since parts of the body
take *o* and the beard takes *a*.

> *Note:* When a word's belonging type surprises you, learn it as a fact of that
> word. The rule gives you the sort for most words on first meeting, and the
> rest arrive one at a time, the way meanings do.

## The Four Sets of Possessive Forms

::: {.examples}
*ʻO laʻu taʻavale.* My car.

*ʻO aʻu taʻavale.* My cars.
:::

Both say the car is mine, and the difference between them is number: one car, or
more than one. Nothing was added for the plural. The form itself changed, from
*laʻu* to *aʻu*.

What changed is the article inside it. *Laʻu* holds three things at once: the
specific article *le*, the belonging word *a*, and the person "my". Take the
article out and *aʻu* is what remains, the belonging word and the person. Samoan
takes it out because a specific plural naming part carries no article at all.
That is the article-less plural you met earlier in this book, where a particular
plural drops *le*, and the possessive form carries that silence inside it.

Two questions therefore decide the form: is the thing one or more than one, and
is it a particular one or any one at all. That gives four forms for each person,
each with an *a*-word version and an *o*-word version.

| The thing possessed | *a*-word form | *o*-word form | English |
|---|---|---|---|
| one particular thing | *laʻu* | *loʻu* | my |
| one thing, any one | *saʻu* | *soʻu* | a ... of mine |
| more than one, particular | *aʻu* | *oʻu* | my |
| more than one, any ones | *ni aʻu* | *ni oʻu* | some ... of mine |

> *Note:* The form *aʻu* is also the word for "me" met earlier in this book. In
> front of a naming word it is "my". Standing on its own it is "me". The form
> beside it in the o-word column looks like the word for "I" met at the start of
> this book, with the catch in a different place: in the word for "I" the catch
> comes first, and in this one it falls between the two vowels.

The article element is what moves down the column. *Laʻu* carries the specific
article *le*, *saʻu* carries the non-specific *se*, *aʻu* carries none, and
*ni aʻu* carries the non-specific plural *ni*. The belonging word and the person
hold their place through all four.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *laʻu taʻavale* | my car |
| *saʻu taʻavale* | a car of mine |
| *aʻu taʻavale* | my cars |
| *ni aʻu taʻavale* | some cars of mine |
| *loʻu fale* | my house |
| *soʻu fale* | a house of mine |
| *oʻu fale* | my houses |
| *ni oʻu fale* | some houses of mine |

### The Missing Article

The same silence shows up with a full-noun owner. One word of the man would
carry the article *le* in front of it. More than one carries nothing, and the
phrase runs straight from the thing to the belonging word:

::: {.examples}
*ʻO ʻupu a le tamāloa.* The words of the man.
:::

**Quick Practice A.** Give the Samoan. Answers below the block.

1. my spoon
2. my spoons
3. my kilt
4. my kilts

Answers: 1. *laʻu sipuni* 2. *aʻu sipuni* 3. *loʻu ʻie* 4. *oʻu ʻie*

## Owners in Pairs and Groups

::: {.examples}
*ʻO le mātou fale.* Our house. (Lit. "the our house")

*ʻO le lā ipu.* Their cup.
:::

When the owner is more than one person, the parts almost never fold into a
single word. The pronoun stays separate, and the article stands in front of it:
*le* for one thing, and nothing at all for more than one. The pronoun is the
same one that goes before a verb, met earlier in this book: *mātou* ("we, but
not you"), *lā*, *tā*, and the rest of the grid.

Look at what is missing from those two phrases. There is no *a* and no *o*.
With a pair or a group of owners the belonging word is not required, and when it
is left out the choice between the two types is not made at all. A cup would be
an *a*-word and a house an *o*-word, and neither phrase above has to say so.

::: {.examples}
*ʻO mātou fale.* Our houses.

*ʻO tā igoa.* The names of us two.
:::

More than one thing and more than one owner, and the naming part is down to the
pronoun and the thing. *Tā* is "we two, you and I", so *ʻo tā igoa* is the pair
of names belonging to the two of us.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *le mātou fale* | our house |
| *mātou fale* | our houses |
| *le lā ipu* | their cup (the two of them) |
| *lā ipu* | their cups (the two of them) |
| *le tā igoa* | our name (yours and mine) |
| *tā igoa* | our names (yours and mine) |
| *le ʻoutou fale* | your house (you all) |
| *le lātou fale* | their house (a group of them) |

> *Note:* The belonging word can still appear alongside a pair or a group of
> owners. It is not required there, and the forms this book prints leave it out.

> **Preview:** An action can be named as a thing, the working, the telling, and
> the person who does it is then joined to it with a belonging word, exactly as
> an owner is joined to a thing. That use of *a* and *o* is completed in
> Chapter 45.

## The For-Word *Mo*

Earlier in this book the with-word *ma* stood one vowel away from the for-word
*mo*, and this chapter completes it:

::: {.examples}
*mo le vaiaso*. for a week

*mo ʻoe*. for you
:::

*Mo* is the **for-word**: it stands in front of a person or a stretch of time
and says who something is for, or how long something lasts.

*Mo* has a partner in *ma*, which also means "for" when a person is handed food
to eat or a thing to keep. *Mo* covers these jobs: the person who benefits from
a state of affairs, the aim of an action, and the length of time something
lasts. The pair partly echoes the belonging split. An older written Samoan had
short forms that look as though they paired *ma* with the *a*-word possessives
and *mo* with the *o*-word ones, and those forms are very rare now. The echo is
a resemblance rather than a rule to lean on: use *mo* for "for", and keep it
apart from the with-word *ma*.

> *Note:* The with-word *ma* and the for-word *mo* stand one vowel apart. *Ma*
> takes a companion along or joins two doers. *Mo* says who something is for.
