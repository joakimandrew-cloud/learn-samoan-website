# Chapter 12: Verbs of Wanting and Seeing

This chapter introduces the two-participant sentence: a verb of seeing,
feeling, wanting, or asking, then the one who does it, then the thing it
reaches, marked by the place word *i*. The pattern points at that thing with
the same *i* that sets an action in a place, as in *Sā alofa le tama i le teine*
(the boy loved the girl).

---

## The Two-Participant Sentence

::: {.examples}
*Sā vaʻai le teine i le iʻa.* The girl saw the fish.

*Sā alofa le tama i le teine.* The boy loved the girl.
:::

The pattern you know best names one participant: who went, who danced, who
stayed in the house. These two name a second. The verb comes first, then the
one who sees or loves, then the thing that is seen or loved. That second thing
is marked by *i*, the place word from earlier in this book.

English treats see, love, and want as verbs that take an object: you see the
fish, you love the girl, the object sits bare right after the verb. English
asking is looser, since you often ask for a thing rather than ask it, but Samoan
treats asking the same as the rest. Samoan hands these verbs no bare object. It
puts the one who does the seeing or asking right after the verb, then points at
the other thing with *i*, the same word that points at a place. You have already
seen *i* mark a place:

::: {.examples}
*Sā ʻou alu i le nuʻu.* I went to the village.

*Sā vaʻai le teine i le iʻa.* The girl saw the fish.
:::

Compare them. Which word points at the village, and which points at the fish?

It is the same word, *i*, doing the same job both times: pointing at what the
verb reaches. In the first sentence the verb is one of moving, so *i* points at
a place. In the second the verb is one of seeing, so *i* points at the thing
seen. The lesson underneath is one to hold onto: in Samoan you do not see the
fish, you see toward it, the way you go toward a village.

The pattern puts the verb first, then its two participants: the one who does the
seeing or feeling, and, marked by *i*, the thing reached.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *Sā vaʻai le teine i le iʻa.* | The girl saw the fish. |
| *Sā matamata le tama i le pusi.* | The boy watched the cat. |
| *Sā alofa le tama i le teine.* | The boy loved the girl. |
| *Sā ʻinoʻino le teine i le ʻiole.* | The girl hated the rat. |

Two of these verbs are for seeing: *vaʻai* (see) and *matamata* (look at,
watch). Two are for feeling: *alofa* (love), carried since early in this book and
now able to join a full sentence, and its opposite *ʻinoʻino* (hate, dislike).
Two of the things these verbs reach are new to you here: *iʻa* (fish) and
*ʻiole* (rat).

Samoan has a third word for looking, *sisila* (look steadily, stare), and this
book asks you only to know what it means. It belongs to the polite speech used
about people of rank, and no one uses that speech about themselves, so no
sentence here is built with it.

All four of the verbs above build the same shape. Whether the thing reached can
be left out depends on the verb rather than on the meaning. With *matamata* it
can be left out. With *vaʻai* it cannot, unless the sentence carries a direction
word, which this book teaches later. With *alofa* it can be left out too, and a
sentence built that way is on record meaning that the person is full of
sympathy. Every sentence in this chapter keeps the thing reached in place.

The one who does the seeing or feeling can also be a preverbal pronoun, the
*ʻou* or *ia* met earlier in this book. It keeps its place in the middle slot
before the verb, and the thing reached still follows with *i*:

::: {.examples}
*Sā ʻou vaʻai i le iʻa.* I saw the fish.

*Sā ia vaʻai i le pusi.* She saw the cat.
:::

> *Note:* The one who sees and the thing reached can come in either order. Both
> *Sā vaʻai le teine i le iʻa* and *Sā vaʻai i le iʻa le teine* say "the girl
> saw the fish". The verb stays first; the two parts after it are not locked in
> place.

## Wanting

Samoan has two wanting words, and this chapter gives each of them one use. To
want to do something, use *fia* in front of the action verb, the word met
earlier in this book: *Sā ʻou fia ʻai* is "I wanted to eat", *Sā ia fia siva* is
"she wanted to dance". To want a thing rather than an action, use *manaʻo*, and
the thing wanted is usually marked by *i*, exactly like the thing seen or loved:

::: {.examples}
*ʻUa manaʻo le ulugāliʻi i le meaʻai.* The couple wanted some food.
:::

*Manaʻo* is the wanting verb for a thing. Here it carries the change particle
*ʻua*, marking the wanting as a state now in place. The couple, *ulugāliʻi*, is
the one who wants; the food, *meaʻai*, is the thing wanted, pointed at with *i*.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *ʻUa manaʻo le ulugāliʻi i le meaʻai.* | The couple wanted some food. |
| *ʻUa manaʻo le tama i le meaʻai.* | The boy wanted some food. |
| *ʻUa manaʻo le teine i le niu.* | The girl wanted a coconut. |

So in the sentences of this chapter, *fia* takes an action and *manaʻo* takes a
thing. Every wanting sentence here still speaks of what someone wanted, in the
past or as a state now reached. Saying want as something happening at this
moment waits for a particle taught later in this book.

## Asking and Calling

Verbs of asking and calling work the same way. The one who asks or calls comes
right after the verb here, though the two parts after the verb are not locked in
place, and the person or thing reached, the one asked or called to, is marked
by *i*:

::: {.examples}
*Sā valaʻau le fafine i le teine.* The woman called to the girl.

*Sā fesili le ofisa i loʻu igoa.* The official asked for my name.
:::

*Valaʻau* is "call", and *i le teine* is who was called to. *Fesili* is "ask",
met earlier in this book; *le ofisa* is the official who asked, and *i loʻu igoa*
is what was asked for, "my name". The verb points at what it reaches with *i*,
whether that is a place, a thing, a person spoken to, or a thing asked about.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *Sā valaʻau le fafine i le teine.* | The woman called to the girl. |
| *Sā valaʻau le tama i le teine.* | The boy called to the girl. |
| *Sā fesili le ofisa i loʻu igoa.* | The official asked for my name. |
| *Sā fesili le tama i loʻu igoa.* | The boy asked for my name. |
| *Sā ʻou valaʻau i le uō.* | I called to the friend. |

## Some of It, Not All

Marking the thing with *i* also carries a shade of meaning. Read this
sentence, built on the verb *ʻai* (eat):

::: {.examples}
*Sā ʻai le teine i le iʻa.* The girl ate some fish.
:::

The fish is marked with *i*, and the sentence says the girl ate some of the
fish, not that she finished it. Pointing at the fish with the place word says
the action reached it, touched it, without saying it was wholly used up. To say
the girl ate the whole fish, Samoan marks the doer with a different word, and
that word draws a real contrast in meaning. The choice is not decorative:
getting it wrong changes what the sentence says.

> **Preview:** The verbs in this chapter point at their target with the place
> word *i*, and a sentence built this way never takes the marker that sets apart
> the doer of a fully completed action. That marker, and the contrast it draws
> with *i*, are completed in Chapter 27. For now, every sentence in this pattern
> uses *i*.

## Words to Learn

**New vocabulary** (memorize these meanings):

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *matamata* | look at, watch |
| *sisila* | look steadily, stare (a polite word, to recognize only) |
| *ʻinoʻino* | hate, dislike |
| *manaʻo* | want (a thing) |
| *valaʻau* | call |
| *iʻa* | fish |
| *ʻiole* | rat |
| *ofisa* | office; official |
| *ulugāliʻi* | couple |

The pattern itself adds no new grammar word. It reuses the place word *i* from
earlier in this book, now pointing at the thing a verb reaches, and the wanting
word *fia*, and it puts *alofa* (love), met before, into a full sentence for the
first time.
