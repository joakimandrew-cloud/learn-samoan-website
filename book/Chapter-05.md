# Chapter 5: The Change and Future Particles

This chapter completes the tense line with two more tense particles, the change
particle *ʻua*, for a change that usually leaves a new state in place, and the
future particle *ʻoleʻā*, and shows you how *ʻua* differs from the two pasts.

---

## The Four Tense Particles

::: {.examples}
*Sā ʻou alu.* I went.

*ʻOleʻā ʻou alu.* I will go.
:::

The frame is the one taught already: a first word that fixes the time, then
the pronoun, then the verb. In the past sentence that first word is *sā*. Swap
it for *ʻoleʻā* and the same going moves into the future. The verb *alu* keeps
its shape, and the pronoun *ʻou* keeps its place. Only the first word changes,
and with it the time of the whole sentence.

That first slot holds a tense particle, and with this chapter it can be filled
four ways: the two pasts *sā* and *na*, the change particle *ʻua*, and the
future *ʻoleʻā*. Here is one sentence run across all four:

| Sāmoan | English |
|---|---|
| *Sā ʻou alu.* | I went (how things were: I was going, I used to go). |
| *Na ʻou alu.* | I went (one trip, begun and finished). |
| *ʻUa ʻou alu.* | I have gone. |
| *ʻOleʻā ʻou alu.* | I will go. |

The first two are the two pasts. Plain English "I went" can stand for both,
which is why the words in brackets matter: *na* marks the going off by its
limits, as one trip begun and finished, and *sā* tells it with no limits in
view, as how things were. Chapter 4 teaches the difference in full. The other two do
different jobs, and this chapter teaches them.

---

## The Change Particle *ʻUa*

::: {.examples}
*ʻUa vevela.* It has become hot.
:::

*ʻUa* is the third word for the first slot, and it does a different job from
the two pasts. It does not send an event back in time. It marks a change, and
reports that a new state is usually in place now. *ʻUa vevela* does not
say it was hot once; it says the weather has turned, and hot is how it is
now. Use *ʻua* whenever something has changed and the new state still stands:

| Sāmoan | English |
|---|---|
| *ʻUa vevela.* | It has become hot. |
| *ʻUa maʻalili.* | It has become cold. |
| *ʻUa ʻou maʻi.* | I have become sick. |
| *ʻUa ʻou pisi.* | I have become busy. |

*Vevela* means "hot", *maʻalili* "cold", and *pisi* "busy". *Maʻi* (sick) is
the state word already met with the two pasts. In each of these sentences the
new state is still in place as you speak. That is the usual case rather than a
rule: the particle reports the change, and the state it leaves need not still
hold at the moment you say it.

> **Preview:** These four are the everyday tense particles, the ones that fix an
> event in time. Other words share that same first slot, and one small set of
> them colours a sentence with things like a wish or a gentle command. Those are
> the mood particles, taught in Chapter 49.

### The Change Particle and the Past

Compare *ʻua* with *sā*. *Sā* places a state back then, in the past; *ʻua* usually
opens a new state that holds now:

::: {.examples}
*Sā ia tāuaso.* She was blind.

*ʻUa ia vaʻai.* Now she sees.
:::

The first sentence, with *sā*, describes the blindness back then. The second,
with *ʻua*, reports the change and the new state that holds now, and together
they tell you the blindness is over. *Vaʻai* means
"see". Sāmoan keeps the two moments apart where English can let them blur.

The two pasts and the change particle can all stand on the same state word.
On *maʻi* the three particles report three different things:

| Sāmoan | English | What it reports |
|---|---|---|
| *Sā ʻou maʻi.* | I was sick (how things were). | the state, in the past |
| *Na ʻou maʻi.* | I fell sick (one event: the sickness began). | the start of the state (one reading *na* can give a state word), as a past event with limits |
| *ʻUa ʻou maʻi.* | I have become sick. | the change, with the new state usually still in place |

*Na* and *ʻua* can both be translated with a beginning ("fell sick", "have
become sick"), but they look at it from different sides. *Na* reports the start
as a past event, marked off by its limits. *ʻUa* reports that a change has
happened and that something new is now the case.

---

## The Future Particle *ʻOleʻā*

::: {.examples}
*ʻOleʻā ia sau.* She will come.
:::

The fourth word for the first slot, *ʻoleʻā*, places the event ahead of now. It
marks something expected to happen, usually quite soon. It fills the slot exactly
as the others do:

| Sāmoan | English |
|---|---|
| *ʻOleʻā ʻou alu.* | I will go. |
| *ʻOleʻā ia sau.* | She will come. |
| *ʻOleʻā mātou siva.* | We will dance. |

*ʻOleʻā* has a shorter form, *ʻā*, that carries the same meaning. You will hear
the short form especially in questions, as in *ʻĀ sau?* for "Is she coming?"
(Lit. "will come?")

## Words to Learn

**New grammar words** (memorize how these work):

| Word | What it does |
|---|---|
| *ʻua* | the change particle: says something has changed and a new state is usually in place now |
| *ʻoleʻā* | the future particle: places the event ahead of now (short form *ʻā*) |

**New vocabulary** (memorize these meanings):

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *vaʻai* | see |
| *vevela* | hot |
| *maʻalili* | cold |
| *pisi* | busy |

**Words to carry** (learned now, used fully later in this book): *fai* covers
"do", "make", "say", and "tell", and it is the most common of the verbs Sāmoan
uses for speaking, but it leans on words for the thing done or said, which come
later in this book.

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *fai* | do, make, say, tell |

1. I will go.
2. It has become cold.
3. She was blind.
4. We will dance.
5. I have become sick.

### Exercise 3: Rewrite with *ʻua*

Each line reports a state that has just set
in. Write the Sāmoan sentence with the change particle.

1. It is hot now.
2. I am busy now.
3. It is cold now.
4. She sees now.
5. I am sick now.

### Exercise 4: Move it into the future

Rewrite each past sentence so the event
is still to come, using *ʻoleʻā*.

1. *Sā ʻou alu.*
2. *Sā ia sau.*
3. *Sā mātou siva.*
4. *Sā lātou alu.*
5. *Sā ʻe siva.*

### Exercise 5: Over, or new now?

Each line describes a state. If the state is over and in the past, write the
sentence with *sā*. If it is new and usually still in place now, write it with
*ʻua*.

1. I was sick, and that is over.
2. I have become sick, and I still am.
3. She was blind, and that is over.
4. She sees now, and did not before.
5. It has become hot, and it still is.
