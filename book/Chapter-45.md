# Chapter 45: The Le-Phrase in Full

This chapter completes the le-phrase by adding the people and things its event
involves, and shows you how to say whose crying, whose feeding, and who fed
whom. The belonging words *a* and *o* do that naming, and inside a le-phrase
they answer to a rule of their own.

---

## The One the Event Is About

::: {.examples}
*le ʻai a namu*. the biting of mosquitoes

*le maualuga o taʻamū*. the height of taʻamū
:::

Both phrases name an event and then name the one it is about. In the first the
biters are *namu* (mosquito), in the second the height belongs to *taʻamū* (a
large edible tuber), the plant that grows in a Samoan garden. Each phrase begins
the way every le-phrase begins, with the article in front of a verb, and each
then adds a belonging word and a noun phrase behind it.

This pattern has four parts in a fixed order: the article *le*, the verb, then
*a* or *o*, then the one the event is about. It is the shape a le-phrase takes
on a verb that carries no agent marker, and everything else in the event keeps
the shape it had in an ordinary sentence. Verbs that do carry one behave
differently, and they come later in this chapter.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *le tagi a le pepe* | the crying of the baby |
| *le tagi a le teine* | the crying of the girl |
| *le alu a le taʻavale* | the going of the car |
| *le ola o le tinā* | the life of the mother |
| *le maualuga o le lāʻau* | the height of the tree |

Earlier in this book a le-phrase stood alone, the staying or the falling with
nobody in it. That phrase was never wrong, and it is still what you use when who
did the thing does not matter. What this chapter adds is the way to put the one
who did it back in.

---

## The Doer Test

A belonging word joined a noun to its owner from the moment you first met *a*
and *o*, and which of the two a noun takes was a fact about that noun. A
*taliga* (ear) is yours in the way a leg or a name is yours, so it takes *o*,
every time:

::: {.examples}
*ʻO le taliga o le tama.* The boy's ear.
:::

Inside a le-phrase the same two words stop working that way for the phrase that
names the one the event is about. That phrase hangs off a verb rather than a
noun, so it has no fixed class to fall back on, and what settles the choice is
the event rather than the words. One question decides it, and the question is
whether the phrase names the doer of something that actually took place. That
question is **the doer test**: if the answer is yes, the word is *a*, and if it
is no, the word is *o*.

Doers in this sense are wider than people. A person or an animal setting out to
do something is a doer, and so is a vehicle, and so is a natural force.

::: {.examples}
*le sasao a le afi*. the flaring up of the fire
:::

The fire is not deciding anything, and Samoan counts it as a doer all the same,
because the flaring up is something the fire itself does. The same goes for the
car in *le alu a le taʻavale* above.

| Phrase | Word | Why |
|---|---|---|
| *le ʻai a namu* | *a* | mosquitoes doing the biting |
| *le alu a le taʻavale* | *a* | a vehicle moving itself |
| *le sasao a le afi* | *a* | a natural force at work |
| *le maualuga o taʻamū* | *o* | a height, which nothing does |
| *le ola o le tinā* | *o* | a life, which nothing does |

*O* covers a second case as well. A speaker who does not want to say outright
that somebody was behind an event reaches for *o*, whether or not that somebody
in fact started it, ran it, or controlled it. The word leaves the question
alone.

The doer test also runs on an event made out of more than one word.
*Taliga tuli* is a deaf ear, a part and a quality standing side by side,
with *tuli* (deaf) behind the noun it describes; put the maker-prefix on the
front and *faʻa=taliga tuli* is turning a deaf ear to something, which is a
thing a person does on purpose.

::: {.examples}
*le faʻa=taliga tuli a le tama*. the boy's turning a deaf ear
:::

> *Note:* Verbs of doing and verbs of natural happening take either word, and
> the choice says whether the doing is being pointed at or left unsaid. When the
> two versions of one phrase were put to Samoan speakers directly and they were
> asked whether both were right, most of them chose *o* and rejected *a*. So
> expect to read both and to hear *o* more often than the rule alone would
> suggest.

> *Note:* The belonging word on a plain noun has not changed. An ear is still an
> *o*-word and a cup is still an *a*-word, and that stays a fact to be learned
> with the noun. The doer test is for the phrase that names the one a le-phrase's
> event is about, and there it settles the choice on its own. A plain owner
> sitting on a noun inside the same le-phrase still takes the word that noun
> takes.

## When the Choice Is Made for You

::: {.examples}
*Sā ita le tinā ʻona ʻo le lē pese o le teine.* The mother was angry because the girl did not sing.
:::

Three words settle the question before the doer test can run, and each of them
forces *o*. The not-word stands inside the le-phrase, between the article and
the verb, where you already know to put it. What matters here is what it does to
the belonging word behind it. Singing that never happened is not something a
doer did, so *a* is out and *o* is the only possibility. The rule behind this is
the doer test running to its end: *a* marks a doer of something that actually
took place, and a negated event did not take place.

::: {.examples}
*le fia pese o le teine*. the girl's wanting to sing

*E mānaia le pese soʻo o le tama.* It is nice that the boy often sings.
:::

The want-word and the often-word close the same door for the same reason.
Wanting to sing is not singing, and singing often is a habit rather than one
event that happened.

| Word | Where it stands | What it forces |
|---|---|---|
| *lē* | between the article and the verb | *o* |
| *fia* | between the article and the verb | *o* |
| *soʻo* | behind the verb | *o* |

These three are the one part of this chapter with no soft edge. Wherever one of
them stands with the verb of a le-phrase, the word on the one the event is about
is *o*, and the doer test is not consulted.

## Verbs That Take Only *O*

::: {.examples}
*le alofa o le tama*. the boy's love

*le fefe o le teine*. the girl's fear
:::

A group of verbs about what goes on inside a person never offers the choice at
all. Loving, being happy, being afraid, being angry, and thinking or feeling
lonely all take *o*, with or without one of the three words above.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *le alofa o le tinā* | the mother's love |
| *le fiafia o le teine* | the girl's happiness |
| *le fefe o le pepe* | the baby's fear |
| *le ita o le tama* | the boy's anger |
| *le manatu o le tinā* | the mother's thinking |

These are states a person is in rather than things a person does, which is why
*a* has nothing to mark. Learn the group as a group and the choice never arises.

## Hearing and Seeing

Hearing and seeing sit between the two halves of this chapter, and Samoan uses
the belonging word to say which kind of hearing or seeing is meant.

::: {.examples}
*ʻUa ʻese le faʻalogo mai a le teine ʻi totonu o le fale.* Inside the house, the girl listened attentively. (Lit. "The listening of the girl inside the house was extraordinary.")
:::

The *a* on *le teine* makes her a doer, and a doer of hearing is someone
listening on purpose. Swap the word for *o* and the hearing becomes something
that merely happened to the person, an accident rather than a thing set out to
be done.

::: {.examples}
*le faʻalogo o le tamaʻitaʻi lea ituʻāiga kī*. the lady's hearing of this kind of key
:::

The lady in that phrase was not listening out for a *kī* (key) of that
*ituʻāiga* (kind, sort). The sound arrived, and *o* is what says so.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *le faʻalogo a le tama* | the boy's listening |
| *le faʻalogo o le tama* | the boy's hearing |
| *le vaʻai a le teine* | the girl's looking |
| *le vaʻai o le teine* | the girl's seeing |

> *Note:* Hearing and seeing are the two verbs the sources show doing this. The
> inside-the-person verbs of the section above take *o* and nothing else, and the
> pair here is the reason that restriction is worth stating: hearing and seeing
> look like members of that group, and they are not.

## What the Event Points At

::: {.examples}
*E alofa le tama ʻi le teine.* The boy cares for the girl.

*ʻO le alofa o le tama ʻi le teine.* The boy caring for the girl.
:::

Some verbs name a feeling pointed at somebody, and the place word *ʻi* carries
what it is pointed at. When the sentence becomes a le-phrase, that part of it
does not move and does not change.

The second line has done one thing to the first. *Le tama* left the slot
behind the verb and came back as *o le tama*, and *ʻi le teine* stayed exactly
where it was, with the place word still on the front of it. That is the rule for
these verbs in its clearest form: the one the event is about becomes a belonging
phrase, and every other part keeps its own shape.

Each sentence below is followed by the le-phrase built from it.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *E alofa le tama ʻi le teine.* | The boy cares for the girl. |
| *ʻO le alofa o le tama ʻi le teine.* | The boy caring for the girl. |
| *E manaʻo le tama ʻi le teine.* | The boy wants the girl. |
| *ʻO le manaʻo o le tama ʻi le teine.* | The boy wanting the girl. |
| *E mātamata le teine ʻi le taʻavale.* | The girl watches the car. |
| *ʻO le mātamata o le teine ʻi le taʻavale.* | The girl watching the car. |

## The Doer That Keeps *E*

Everything so far has been built on verbs that take no agent marker. With a verb
that does take one, the le-phrase behaves differently.

::: {.examples}
*E faʻa=lē=lelei le faʻa=susu fagu e tinā palagi o pepe.* The palagi mothers' feeding of the babies with bottles is not good.
:::

The agent marker stays on the doer where it was. Two phrases hang off the verb
*faʻa=susu fagu*, bottle-feeding, which is built on *fagu* (bottle). The doers
are *e tinā palagi*, the *palagi* (European, white person) mothers, carrying the
agent marker they would carry in an ordinary sentence. The babies being fed are
*o pepe*, a belonging phrase with *o*: where the thing a transitive event is
done to is a belonging phrase, the word on it is *o* and never *a*. Either
phrase may come first, and both orders are ordinary Samoan.

| Part | Form | Function |
|---|---|---|
| the article | *le* | heads the le-phrase |
| the verb | *faʻa=susu fagu* | names the event |
| the agent marker | *e tinā palagi* | names the doer |
| the belonging word | *o pepe* | names the ones it was done to |

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *le fafaga e le tinā o le pepe* | the mother's feeding of the baby |
| *le fafaga o le pepe e le tinā* | the mother's feeding of the baby |

Now drop the ones it was done to and read these two:

::: {.examples}
*E faʻa=lē=lelei le faʻa=susu fagu e tinā palagi.* The palagi mothers' feeding with bottles is not good.

*E faʻa=lē=lelei le faʻa=susu fagu a tinā palagi.* The palagi mothers' feeding with bottles is not good.
:::

Compare them. Which word changed?

One, and it is the word in front of *tinā palagi*. Both sentences mean the same
thing, and both are correct. With nobody named as the one it was done to, the
doer may keep the agent marker *e* or take the belonging word *a* instead, and
the sentence says the same either way. The choice reopens only because the *o*
phrase is gone; put the babies back in and the doer goes back to *e*, unless the
doer is the one who owns them, which is a shape still to come in this chapter.

## The Thing the Event Happens To

Inside a le-phrase, the one an event is done to can take either of two shapes,
and the common one is the belonging phrase with *o*.

::: {.examples}
*ʻO le fafaga o le pepe ʻi le fagu susu.* The feeding of the baby with a bottle of milk.
:::

*Le pepe* is what the feeding is done to, and *o* marks it, with the place word
carrying the bottle it was done with. Where that slot is filled by a belonging
phrase, the word on it is *o*, whatever the verb.

The second shape is one you have built already. Where a le-phrase followed a
do-not verb earlier in this book, what the event was done to sat behind the verb
as a plain noun phrase, with no belonging word at all:

::: {.examples}
*ʻAua le maumau=ina le taimi.* Do not waste time.
:::

*Le taimi* is what would be wasted, and it stands bare. That shape is correct
and you will meet it, and it is the less frequent of the two, so read *o* as the
ordinary way of saying this and the bare noun phrase as the other one.

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *le tapē o le puaʻa* | the killing of the pig |
| *le fafaga o le pepe* | the feeding of the baby |
| *le fafaga o le puaʻa* | the feeding of the pig |

## The Owner-as-Doer Pattern

There is one more shape, and it completes something promised earlier in this
book. It is what Samoan can do when the doer of an event owns the thing the
event happens to.

::: {.examples}
*Sā inu le susu a le tama.* The boy drank the milk. (Lit. "The milk of the boy was drunk.")
:::

No agent marker appears there. The boy is named by a belonging phrase sitting on
the milk, and when no other doer is named, a sentence built this way is usually
taken to mean that the owner is the one who did it. That is **the owner-as-doer
pattern**, and inside a le-phrase it stacks two belonging phrases in a row:

::: {.examples}
*E ʻese le vave o le tapē o le puaʻa a le teine.* The girl killed her pig extraordinarily fast. (Lit. "The fastness of the killing of the pig of the girl is extraordinary.")
:::

Read the stack from the verb outward. *Le tapē* is the killing, *o le puaʻa*
names what was killed, and *a le teine* names who the pig belongs to; with
nobody else named, she is read as the one who did the killing. The first
belonging word is always *o*, because the first phrase is what the event
happened to. The second one is *a* or *o* by the ordinary rule for that noun and
its owner, the rule you learned for belonging, because the second phrase is a
plain owner and not an event at all.

::: {.examples}
*le kī=ina o le leitio a le tama*. the boy's switching on of the radio (Lit. "the switching on of the radio of the boy")
:::

The radio is an *a*-word for its owner, so the second belonging word is *a*; the
first is *o* because the radio is what was switched on.

| Part | Form | Function |
|---|---|---|
| the verb | *le tapē* | names the event |
| the first phrase | *o le puaʻa* | what the event happened to, always *o* |
| the second phrase | *a le teine* | the owner, read as the doer |

## Words to Learn

**New grammar words** (memorize how these work):

| Word | What it does |
|---|---|
| *a* | the belonging word, met earlier: inside a le-phrase it marks the one the event is about as the doer of something that took place |
| *o* | the belonging word, met earlier: inside a le-phrase it marks a non-doer, or leaves the doing unsaid, and it is the word on what a transitive event happened to whenever that stands as a belonging phrase |
| *e* | the agent marker, met earlier: it stays on the doer inside a le-phrase whenever the thing done to is marked with *o* and the doer is not that thing's owner |
| *lē* | the not-word, met earlier: inside a le-phrase it forces *o* |
| *fia* | the want-word, met earlier: inside a le-phrase it forces *o* |
| *soʻo* | the often-word, met earlier: inside a le-phrase it forces *o* |

**New vocabulary** (memorize these meanings):

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| *tuli* | deaf |
| *taliga* | ear |
| *fagu* | bottle |
| *namu* | mosquito |
| *taʻamū* | taʻamū (a large edible tuber) |
| *palagi* | European, white person |
| *ituʻāiga* | kind, sort |
| *kī* | key |
