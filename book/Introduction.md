# Introduction: Sounds, Spelling, and How Words Work {.unnumbered}

Before the course begins, this chapter teaches the sound system of Samoan:
how to say any written word aloud with the stress in the right place, how to
read the two marks that carry meaning, how to cope when a printed page leaves
those marks out, and which style of spoken Samoan this book teaches. None of
it needs to be memorized like vocabulary: it is a short set of habits, and
every chapter that follows will exercise them.

---

## Where This Book Comes From

This course was built from the modern reference grammar of Samoan, Mosel and
Hovdhaugen's *Samoan Reference Grammar*, cross-checked against Churchward's
1951 *A Samoan Grammar*, and spelled to Allardice's
*A Simplified Dictionary of Modern Samoan*.
Its sentences are drawn from those works and from two
graded courses, *Say it in Samoan* and Hunkin's *Gagana Sāmoa*. Every Samoan
sentence in this book is traceable to one of those sources, and nothing was
invented. Where the experts hedge, this book hedges too: when you read
"usually" here, it is because the grammar says usually.

Two promises follow from that. First, this book always prints the two
pronunciation marks you are about to meet, even though much of the Samoan you
will see in the world does not. Second, the Samoan taught here is the careful,
formal style, the one used in writing and in schools; the everyday spoken
style is different, and this chapter tells you how.

---

## The Letters and Their Sounds

Samoan is written with letters you already know. The vowels are a, e, i, o,
u. The consonants are f, g, l, m, n, p, s, t, v, joined by h, k, and r, which
appear mainly in words borrowed from other languages. One more consonant is
written not with a letter but with a mark, ʻ, and it has a section of its own
below.

Say the five vowels aloud, one after another: a, e, i, o, u. Hearing your own
voice move between them, before any word puts them to work, is a good first
step toward the ear this book keeps asking you to use.

The letter g is the one surprise: it is pronounced like the ng in English
"song", never like the g in "go", and unlike English ng it can begin a word.

Every vowel comes in a short form and a long form. The long form is usually the same
sound held longer, and it is written with a line over the letter: ā, ē, ī, ō,
ū. That line is one of the two marks this book always prints, and the next
section shows you why it matters.

---

## Long and Short Vowels

Vowel length carries meaning. Many pairs of Samoan words differ by nothing but
the length of one vowel, and they mean different things:

| Samoan | English |
|---|---|
| *tama* | child, boy |
| *tamā* | father |
| *fulu* | wash, clean |
| *fulū* | influenza, cold |
| *tala* | story |
| *tālā* | dollar |

Say the first pair aloud. What changed between them? Only the length of the
final vowel, and with it the whole meaning: hold the a short and you have
said "child", hold it long and you have said "father". Samoan ears rely on
that difference; start practicing it now.

---

## Where the Stress Falls

In most Samoan words, the stress lands on the second-to-last syllable: in
*tama*, the first a; in *tautala* (speak), the middle a. Most of the time a
syllable is one vowel, so counting back two vowels finds the stress. Where it
does not is when two vowels sit side by side: most such pairs are two syllables
and count as two, but a few of them are said as one syllable when they fall in
that second-to-last place, and then the pair carries the stress together. When
the last vowel of a word is long, it draws the stress to itself instead: in
*tamā*, the final ā.

Read those as habits rather than as laws. A small number of words are stressed
a syllable further back than the habit predicts, and some word endings pull the
stress to a place of their own. So take each new word slowly, find its stress
by ear, and say it aloud rather than working it out from the spelling.

> *Note:* At the end of a word, short e and short i sit very close together
> for an English ear: *ate* (liver) against *ati* (to build). Spelling will
> not train the difference; hearing it side by side, spoken aloud, will.

## The Two Marks

The first mark you have already met: the line over a long vowel, as in *tamā*.
Its Samoan name is the *faʻamamafa*; this book calls it the long-vowel mark.

The second is the ʻ you saw among the consonants, as in *faʻamamafa* itself.
It is written as a turned comma, and its Samoan name is the *koma liliu*, the
turned comma; this book calls it the ʻ mark. It stands for a catch in the
voice, the same break you make in the middle of English "uh-oh", and it
counts as a full consonant, exactly as real as an f or a t. Compare
*ava* (passage in the reef) with *ʻava* (beard): the only difference is that
catch at the front.

These are the two pronunciation marks this book uses, and you now know both.
Every Samoan word in this book carries them in full. Other writers on Samoan
mark more than this: a grammar may add its own signs for a short vowel or for
where the stress falls.

### The Marks That Vanish

Most of the Samoan you will meet,
in newspapers and in most books by Samoan authors, is printed without either
mark. That spelling is the generally accepted modern one in Samoa itself; the
marks are added only occasionally, to pull apart words that would otherwise
be confused. The school books and children's books published by the education
departments in Pagopago and Honolulu still print the marks, and so do some
Samoan children's books published in New Zealand, as this book does.

So the same bare letters can hide several words. The bare letters m, a, l, o
may be *malō* (hard, firm) or *mālō* (to win, a victory, or
the government). The bare letters s, a may be *sa* (a
word for "a") or *sā*, the word that stamps a whole sentence as past, and one
of the first words this book teaches. Samoan readers meet this every day and
resolve it from context, the way you read the English word "lead" without
noticing it could be a metal or a verb.

Expect the ambiguity, and treat reading unmarked Samoan as a skill this book
will keep training. The marked spelling you learn here is your anchor: because
this book always prints the marks, you will always know which word you are
learning, and when the marks vanish on a real page you will know what the
possibilities are. Later chapters return to unmarked text as the words pile
up.

## The Falling Question Tune

Samoan runs opposite to an English instinct in one more place: the voice. An
English yes or no question lifts the voice at the end. A Samoan yes or no
question does not lift it: usually the voice falls, and stays low over the
last two or three syllables. In the chapters that teach questions, this book
marks that falling tune with the arrow ↘ after the question. You will meet it
in the very first sentences of the course; from now on, when you see ↘, keep
the ending low.

## The Two Ways Samoan Is Spoken

Samoan has two named speech styles, generally considered two pronunciation
systems: *tautala lelei*, the careful style this book teaches, and
*tautala leaga*, the everyday style you will hear. The careful style is the basis of
written Samoan and of teaching in schools, and it is the style a learner is
expected to use. Even so, Samoans will sometimes use the everyday style with a
foreigner, or switch to it once you have been talking a while. The everyday
style folds some of its sounds together: t is said as k, n is said as g (the ng
sound), and where the careful style has r, the everyday style has l. A catch at
the start of a word is normally dropped there as well, so the mark you have just
learned to hear often goes unsaid.

The folding runs one way only: the careful style keeps distinctions that the
everyday style erases, and never the reverse. So the skill you will build is
recognition, hearing through the everyday sounds back to the careful forms
you learned. You never need to produce the everyday style yourself. This book
teaches one Samoan; your ear will learn to recognize two.

## A Word Is What It Does

One principle runs through the whole language, and it is told here once. In
an English dictionary, a word usually arrives with its class attached: noun,
verb, and so on. In Samoan, a great many word roots, perhaps the majority, can
serve in more than one role: the same word can name a thing in one sentence
and be the action of another. A word
takes its class from its slot in the sentence, not from a label in the
dictionary. So when this book says "the verb of this sentence", it is
describing the job the word is doing there, not a permanent badge the word
wears.

## Five Shapes of Sentence

Samoan builds its clauses, and so its simplest sentences, in five structural
shapes. You do not need their names now: each is taught in its own place later
in this book. The simplest shape is a sentence built on a verb; it is where the
course begins, and you will be building real ones from the very first pages.

## How to Use This Book

Each chapter adds one new thing and drills it. Work in order: later chapters
stand on earlier ones, and nothing is used before it is taught. Every chapter
has a workbook of its own for practice and a short quiz to check yourself;
do the exercises on paper, and check the answer key only when a section is
finished.

Above all, say every Samoan word and sentence out loud, not in your head. The
long vowels, the catch of the ʻ mark, the stress, and the falling question
tune live only in the voice, and the voice learns them only by doing.
